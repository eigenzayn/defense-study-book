A. Thesis

Thesis: Data-based Numerical Simulation of Buoyant Jet Flow. Supervisor: Prof. Stefan Pirker. Every number here is taken from the thesis of 10 October 2026. Equation and figure numbers refer to that version; the derivations follow Appendix D of the thesis.

The talk in 15 minutes

Explain. rCFD replays a recorded flow and moves a scalar with it. It was built for passive scalars, which do not change the flow. Temperature does change the flow. So I measured what the replay loses and how much I can repair without solving the flow again.

Numbers. Reference 569 min. One replay 2.16 min on average. About 260 times faster.

Core. Thermal processes run for hours, and full CFD of that time is too slow for daily use.

Explain. rCFD is 260 times faster in my case. But a fast model is only useful if we know its error. My thesis measures this error for a case where temperature matters, and it shows which part can be repaired.

Summary: Fast is useful only with a known error.

A3 Explain recurrence CFD in simple words.PREDICTED

your thesis: everything you used can be asked

Core. A normal CFD run is recorded once, and the replay reuses the recorded motion again and again.

Explain. The preparation solves the full equations and stores, frame by frame, which share of each cell moves to which cell. The replay applies these stored maps to the temperature. Velocity and pressure are never solved again, so it is cheap. One replay step has four stages.

Derivation
\[\begin{align*} \mathbf{T}^{*} &= \mathbf{M}^{(f_n)}\,\mathbf{T}^{n} \why{recorded map: move the heat} \\ \mathbf{T}^{**} &= \mathcal{S}(\mathbf{T}^{*}) \why{face exchange: add mixing} \\ \mathbf{T}^{***} &= \mathcal{C}(\mathbf{T}^{**}) \why{correction: my work} \\ \mathbf{T}^{n+1} &= \mathcal{B}(\mathbf{T}^{***}) \why{conservation constraint: fix the total} \end{align*}\]

Together this is Eq. (2.10), $\mathbf{T}^{n+1} = \mathcal{B}(\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{S}(\mathbf{M}\mathbf{T}^n)))$. Standard rCFD has $\mathcal{C} = \text{identity}$.

Summary: Record once, replay many times. Map, swap, correct, balance.

Source: Thesis, Sections 2.2–2.7, Eq. (2.10).

A4 Why is this AI, and not only CFD?ASKED BEFORE

thesis part, Küng committee (“Why and how does ML solve this problem?”)

Core. It is a data-based simulation: an expensive solver produces data, and a cheap model reuses it.

Explain. The database plays the role of training data. Choosing the next frame is a nearest-neighbour search in a database of states, the method of analogues. My main finding is about generalization: a database belongs to a flow regime. A boundary condition that changes the regime makes the database out of distribution.

Summary: Database = training data. New regime = out of distribution.

Source: Thesis, Section 2.2 and Section 6.4.

A5 What is the recurrence matrix, and is your flow recurrent?PREDICTED

your thesis: everything you used can be asked

Core. The matrix compares every pair of recorded states, and my flow is not recurrent.

Explain. A small distance means two states are similar, so the replay could jump between them. In my tank the matrix has two blocks, filling and settling, and no state comes back. So the replay plays the frames in order. It is still rCFD, but the saving comes only from not solving velocity and pressure.

Numbers. Median prominence 0.028. The method asks for 0.05 to 0.1.

Derivation
\[\begin{align*} R_{fg} &= \bigl\lVert \Phi^{(f)} - \Phi^{(g)} \bigr\rVert \why{distance between frames $f$ and $g$, Eq.~(2.5)} \\ d(f,g) &= R_{fg}/\max R \why{normalise to $[0,1]$, Eq.~(2.6)} \\ P_{\mathrm{rel}} &= \bigl\langle d_{\max} - d_{\min} \bigr\rangle / \bar{d} \why{depth of the dips over the plateau, Eq.~(2.7)} \end{align*}\]

Summary: Two blocks, no return, 0.028 $\to$ frames in order.

Source: Thesis, Section 2.4, Figure 2.2.

A6 What is the face exchange, and what is $\fsd$?PREDICTED

your thesis: everything you used can be asked

Core. After each advection the library moves a share of heat across every face, from warm to cold.

Explain. A pure displacement does not mix, so this step adds mixing. $\fsd$ is its strength per step. It has no unit, so it is not a diffusion coefficient, but it can be translated into one. The exchange can only reduce differences. It can never rebuild a smeared front.

Numbers. $\fsd = 0.5$ gives $2.5\times10^{-7}$ m$^2$/s, ten times the molecular value.

Derivation
\[\begin{align*} \Delta_{\mathrm{swap}} &= \fsd\,V\,\frac{T_{c_1}-T_{c_0}}{2} \why{exchange of Eq.~(2.11)} \\ Q_{\mathrm{swap}} &= \rho c_p\,\fsd\,c_s^{3}\,\frac{T_{c_1}-T_{c_0}}{2} \why{heat of that amount, $V = c_s^3$} \\ Q_{\mathrm{Fick}} &= \rho c_p\,D\,\frac{T_{c_1}-T_{c_0}}{c_s}\,c_s^{2}\,\Delta t \why{Fick's law over one face and one step} \\ D_{\mathrm{eq}} &= \frac{\fsd\,c_s^{2}}{2\,\Delta t} \why{set $Q_{\mathrm{Fick}} = Q_{\mathrm{swap}}$, Eq.~(2.13)} \end{align*}\]

Summary: Swap = one-way mixing. Converts to $D_{\mathrm{eq}} = \fsd c_s^2/2\Delta t$.

Source: Thesis, Section 2.6, Eqs. (2.11)–(2.13); Appendix D.1.

A7 Why is there no single best mixing constant?PREDICTED

your thesis: everything you used can be asked

Core. The two measures want different amounts of mixing, because the outlet sits just below the front.

Explain. The mean temperature is best with little mixing. The height of the heat is best near $\fsd = 0.5$. The more the replay smears the front, the more warm fluid reaches the outlet and leaves the tank.

Numbers. Mean-temperature error 1.07 K at little mixing. At $\fsd = 3$ the tank ends 5.08 K too cold.

Summary: Outlet below the front $\to$ smearing drains heat.

Source: Thesis, Section 5.1.

A8 Why not simply use negative diffusion to sharpen the front?PREDICTED

your thesis: everything you used can be asked

Core. Negative diffusion is ill posed: small wiggles grow fastest.

Explain. It does steepen the front, but it also amplifies every disturbance at the grid scale. In my 1D test a disturbance of 1 mK left the range of the data by more than 1 K after 21 steps. So I keep the useful part and stop the dangerous part with a limiter.

Derivation
\[\begin{align*} T_i^{n+1} &= T_i^n + \nu\,(T_{i+1}^n - 2T_i^n + T_{i-1}^n) \why{explicit diffusion step} \\ T_i^n &= G^n e^{\mathrm{i}k i\Delta x} \why{one Fourier mode} \\ G &= 1 + \nu\,(2\cos k\Delta x - 2) = 1 - 4\nu\sin^2(k\Delta x/2) \why{insert the mode} \\ G &= 1 + 4\lvert\nu\rvert \why{shortest mode $k\Delta x = \pi$, $\nu<0$} \end{align*}\]

$\lvert G\rvert > 1$, so the shortest mode grows in every step.

Summary: Negative diffusion grows wiggles. Keep the steepening, limit the rest.

Source: Thesis, Section 4.2.2, Eq. (4.13), Figure 4.1.

A9 Explain your limiter.PREDICTED

your thesis: everything you used can be asked

Core. Each face may move heat up the gradient only as far as both cells stay inside their local bounds.

Explain. For every cell I take the smallest and the largest value of the cell and its neighbours. Across a face, heat moves from the colder to the warmer cell. The amount is the smaller of the two headrooms, times a strength $s$. This is the limiter idea of flux-corrected transport, by Zalesak.

Derivation
\[\begin{align*} Q_h^{+} &= C_h\,(T_h^{\max} - T_h),\quad Q_c^{-} = C_c\,(T_c - T_c^{\min}) \why{headrooms, Eq.~(4.15)} \\ \Delta q_f &= s\,\min(Q_h^{+},Q_c^{-}) \why{limited exchange, Eq.~(4.16)} \\ C_i\,\Delta T_i &\le n_f\,s\,Q_i^{+} \why{at most $n_f$ faces bring heat} \\ T_i + \Delta T_i &\le T_i^{\max} \quad\text{if}\quad s \le 1/n_f = 1/6 \why{Eq.~(4.17)} \end{align*}\]

Summary: Smaller headroom times $s$. Conservative, no new extremum, safe for $s \le 1/6$.

Source: Thesis, Section 4.2.3; Appendix D.4.

A10 Why the direction weight?PREDICTED

your thesis: everything you used can be asked

Core. The front is horizontal, so the correction should act across it and not along it.

Explain. Each face gets the weight $\cos^2$ of the angle between its link and the vertical. Faces across the front get one, faces along it get zero. Without the weight the correction roughens the front sideways. This weight is exactly the face coefficient of a diffusion that acts in one direction only.

Derivation
\[\begin{align*} K_f &= \hat{\mathbf{e}}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{K}\,\hat{\mathbf{e}} \why{two-point face coefficient} \\ &= K\,(\hat{\mathbf{e}}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}})^2 \why{rank-one tensor $\mathbf{K} = K\hat{\mathbf{n}}\hat{\mathbf{n}}^{\mathsf{T}}$} \\ &= K\,w^{\mathrm{dir}}_f \why{Eq.~(4.21)} \end{align*}\]

Summary: Act across the front: $\cos^2$ weight = one-direction diffusion.

Source: Thesis, Section 4.2.4, Eqs. (4.19), (4.21).

A11 The recorded diffusivity did not help. Why?PREDICTED

your thesis: everything you used can be asked

Core. The recorded values are larger than the explicit exchange can apply, so every face hits the cap.

Explain. The library exchanges at most half a cell volume per face and step. The recorded diffusivity asks for about nine times more. So every face exchanges the maximum, and the field acts exactly like the constant $\fsd = 0.5$. The replays are bit-identical, which proves it. A shorter step or an implicit exchange would let the field act.

Derivation
\[\begin{align*} 4 \times \tfrac18 &= \tfrac12 \why{at most four loops of 1/8 of a cell} \\ \mathrm{Di} &= D\,\Delta t/c_s^{2} \le 0.5 \why{the cap as a diffusion number, Eq.~(2.16)} \end{align*}\]

Summary: Recorded $D$ above the cap $\to$ acts like a constant.

Source: Thesis, Section 5.2.1, Figure 5.4.

A12 What happened under the cooled lid?PREDICTED

your thesis: everything you used can be asked

Core. On the fluid of the reference, the corrected replay ends 0.06 K from the reference.

Explain. The reference fluid has the specific heat 1006.43 J/(kg K). A replay given the specific heat of water cools four times less for the same wall power, and ends 4.34 K too warm. On the right fluid the replay is close, and the correction puts the heat back in the right layer.

Numbers. Cooling depth 9.62 K. Without correction $-0.66$ K. With correction at $s\approx0.55$: $+0.06$ K, end profile 0.41 K.

Derivation
\[\begin{align*} \Delta T_{\mathrm{cell}} &= -\frac{q\,\Delta t}{\rho\,c_p\,V} \why{wall loss of one cell, Eq.~(4.25)} \\ \frac{\Delta T_{\mathrm{rep}}}{\Delta T_{\mathrm{case}}} &= \frac{c_{\mathrm{case}}}{c_{\mathrm{rep}}} = \frac{1006.43}{4182} = \frac{1}{4.155} \why{same power, two specific heats} \\ h_{\mathrm{eff}} &= G\,h\,\frac{c_{\mathrm{case}}}{c_{\mathrm{rep}}} \why{Eq.~(4.26)} \end{align*}\]

Summary: Same fluid on both sides first. Then the correction does the rest.

Source: Thesis, Sections 6.1–6.2; Appendix D.5.

A13 What could not be repaired, and why?PREDICTED

your thesis: everything you used can be asked

Core. While the tank fills, the layers separate too slowly, and no local rule can fix that.

Explain. A local correction only moves heat between neighbours, so it can change the shape of the front but not carry heat from one layer to another. This is missing transport. A vertical flux read from the recording supplies that transport. It reduces the error, but the tank then ends too cold.

Numbers. Heterogeneity error over the warm inflow 2.25 K $\to$ 0.45 K. End 0.86 K too cold.

Derivation
\[\begin{align*} \rho c_p v T &= \rho c_p v\,\bar{T}_{\mathrm{b}} + \rho c_p v\,(T - \bar{T}_{\mathrm{b}}) \why{split $T$ at the band mean} \\ F &= \rho c_p v\,(T - \bar{T}_{\mathrm{b}}) \why{the recorded flux, Eq.~(2.18)} \\ \langle F\rangle_{\mathrm{b}} &= \rho c_p \langle v' T'\rangle_{\mathrm{b}} \why{band mean: the eddy heat flux} \end{align*}\]

Summary: Local repair fixes shape. Missing transport needs recorded transport.

Source: Thesis, Sections 6.5–6.6; Appendix D.2.

Core. Every replay is deterministic, and every run changes one setting against a control.

Explain. A rerun gives the same trace, bit for bit. Replay and reference are compared at the same instants and over the same end window. The energy budget of each case is closed term by term.

Numbers. End window 580 to 599 s on both sides.

A15 Why these error measures? Bias and RMSE?PREDICTED

your thesis: everything you used can be asked

Core. The mean temperature says how much heat there is; the profile and the front width say where it is.

Explain. A replay can be right on one and wrong on the other, so I report both. On a trace, the bias gives the side and the RMSE gives the size. Their identity separates an offset from a wrong shape.

Derivation
\[\begin{align*} \mathrm{RMSE}^2 &= \frac1N\sum_k e_k^2 \why{definition, Eq.~(4.3)} \\ &= \frac1N\sum_k \bigl[(e_k-\mathrm{bias}) + \mathrm{bias}\bigr]^2 \why{add and subtract the bias} \\ &= \sigma_e^2 + 2\,\mathrm{bias}\cdot 0 + \mathrm{bias}^2 \why{$\sum_k (e_k - \mathrm{bias}) = 0$} \\ &= \mathrm{bias}^2 + \sigma_e^2 \end{align*}\]

Summary: How much heat versus where it is. RMSE$^2$ = bias$^2$ + spread$^2$.

Source: Thesis, Section 4.1.2, Eq. (4.3).

A16 Why is the flow laminar?PREDICTED

your thesis: everything you used can be asked

Core. The jet enters at a Reynolds number of about 99, far below transition.

Explain. So the equations are solved as they stand, with no turbulence model. The case file confirms that the turbulence model is off. Buoyancy is slightly stronger than inertia, so the jet bends up within a few inlet widths.

Numbers. $Re = UD/\nu \approx 99$ with $U = 0.02$ m/s, $D = 5$ mm. $Ri = 1.84$.

Summary: $Re \approx 99$: laminar. $Ri = 1.84$: the jet bends up.

Source: Thesis, Section 3.3, Table 3.2.

A17 Why the Boussinesq approximation?PREDICTED

your thesis: everything you used can be asked

Core. The density changes by only 1.5 per cent, so it matters only where gravity acts.

Explain. Density is kept constant except in the buoyancy term. This buoyancy term is exactly the link that the replay cuts: the replay keeps the recorded motion, so the temperature can no longer change the velocity.

Derivation
\[\begin{align*} \rho\,\mathbf{g} &\approx \rho_0\,\mathbf{g}\bigl(1 - \beta(T - T_0)\bigr) \why{linear in $T$ for small changes} \\ &= \rho_0\,\mathbf{g} - \rho_0\,\beta\,(T-T_0)\,\mathbf{g} \why{second term = buoyancy} \end{align*}\]

Summary: Constant density, except where gravity sees it. The replay cuts this term.

Source: Thesis, Section 2.1, Eqs. (2.1)–(2.3).

Empirical risk minimization and generalization

B1 Write down a supervised data set and the training framework.ASKED BEFORE

Klambauer (several students), Berger-Weber committee

Core. We have $N$ pairs of input and label, and learning means minimizing the average loss on them.

Explain. The pairs are drawn independently from an unknown distribution. A model maps an input to a prediction, and a loss says how wrong one prediction is. The average loss on the training data is the empirical risk.

Derivation
\[\begin{align*} Z &= \{(\mathbf{x}_n,y_n)\}_{n=1}^{N} \why{training data, i.i.d. from $p(\mathbf{x},y)$} \\ \Remp(\mathbf{w}) &= \frac1N\sum_{n=1}^{N} L\bigl(y_n, g(\mathbf{x}_n;\mathbf{w})\bigr) \why{average loss} \\ \hat{\mathbf{w}} &= \arg\min_{\mathbf{w}} \Remp(\mathbf{w}) \why{learning} \end{align*}\]

Summary: Data, model, loss, average, minimize.

Source: DLNN script WS24, Ch. 3 and 5.1.

B2 What is the generalization error? Why can we not minimize it directly?ASKED BEFORE

Klambauer (Fürnkranz committee), Schedl committee

Core. It is the expected loss on new data, and we cannot compute it because the true distribution is unknown.

Explain. So we minimize the empirical risk instead and estimate the generalization error on data we did not train on: a test set or cross-validation. The training loss is not a fair estimate, because the weights were chosen on that data.

Derivation
\[\begin{align*} \Rgen(\mathbf{w}) &= \mathbb{E}_{(\mathbf{x},y)\sim p}\bigl[L(y,g(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{w}))\bigr] \why{expected loss} \\ &= \int L\bigl(y,g(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{w})\bigr)\,p(\mathbf{x},y)\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\,\mathrm{d}y \why{needs $p$, which is unknown} \\ &\approx \frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M} L\bigl(y_m,g(\mathbf{x}_m;\mathbf{w})\bigr) \why{test set, law of large numbers} \end{align*}\]

Summary: Risk = loss on unseen data. Estimate it with a test set or cross-validation.

Source: DLNN script WS24, Ch. 5.2–5.3.

B3 What is a loss function? Give examples.ASKED BEFORE

Klambauer, March 2026 (“not all losses are differentiable, 0–1 loss”)

Core. A loss compares a label with a prediction and is zero when the prediction is right.

Explain. It is never negative. We choose it to fit the task. Not every loss can be trained with gradient descent: the 0–1 loss has no useful gradient, so we use cross-entropy instead.

Explain. Examples: squared loss $\tfrac12(y-\hat y)^2$ for regression; binary cross-entropy $-y\log\hat y - (1-y)\log(1-\hat y)$; categorical cross-entropy $-\sum_k y_k\log\hat y_k$; hinge loss $\max(0, 1 - y\,g(\mathbf{x}))$ for the SVM.

Summary: Non-negative, zero when right. 0–1 loss $\to$ use cross-entropy.

Source: DLNN script WS24, Sec. 4.1–4.4.

B4 Show ERM on linear regression.ASKED BEFORE

Klambauer (Küng committee), Schedl committee

Core. With a linear model and squared loss the risk is convex, so the minimum has a closed form.

Explain. Set the gradient to zero and solve. Gradient descent reaches the same point step by step. For one sample the gradient is error times input, and the same pattern comes back in backprop.

Derivation
\[\begin{align*} \Remp(\mathbf{w}) &= \tfrac12\lVert \mathbf{y} - X\mathbf{w}\rVert^2 \why{squared loss, all samples} \\ \nabla_{\mathbf{w}}\Remp &= -X^{\mathsf{T}}(\mathbf{y} - X\mathbf{w}) \why{chain rule} \\ 0 &= X^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{y} - X^{\mathsf{T}}X\,\hat{\mathbf{w}} \why{gradient zero at the minimum} \\ \hat{\mathbf{w}} &= (X^{\mathsf{T}}X)^{-1}X^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{y} \why{normal equations, pseudo-inverse} \end{align*}\]

Summary: Convex $\to$ closed form. Gradient = error $\times$ input.

Source: DLNN script WS24, Sec. 4.1.3–4.1.4.

B5 Overfitting, underfitting, bias and variance.PREDICTED

DLNN script Sec. 5.4; bias and MSE were asked only in signal processing (Huemer)

Core. Too simple a model has high bias, too complex a model has high variance.

Explain. An underfitting model cannot follow the data. An overfitting model follows the noise, so the training error is low and the test error is high. Very large networks show double descent: after the point where they fit the training data exactly, the test error falls again.

Derivation
\[\begin{align*} y &= f(\mathbf{x}) + \varepsilon,\quad \mathbb{E}[\varepsilon] = 0,\ \mathrm{Var}[\varepsilon] = \sigma^2 \why{data model} \\ \mathbb{E}\bigl[(y-\hat g)^2\bigr] &= \mathbb{E}\bigl[(f + \varepsilon - \bar g + \bar g - \hat g)^2\bigr] \why{add and subtract $\bar g = \mathbb{E}[\hat g]$} \\ &= \sigma^2 + (f - \bar g)^2 + \mathbb{E}\bigl[(\hat g - \bar g)^2\bigr] \why{cross terms have zero mean} \\ &= \text{noise} + \text{bias}^2 + \text{variance} \end{align*}\]

Summary: Simple $\to$ bias. Complex $\to$ variance. Large nets: double descent.

Source: DLNN script WS24, Sec. 5.4.

From one neuron to a deep network

B6 Write the formula of a neuron.ASKED BEFORE

Schlüter

Core. A neuron takes a weighted sum of its inputs plus a bias, and passes it through a non-linear function.

Explain. The weighted sum is the pre-activation, and the result is the activation. A layer does this for many neurons at once. The forward pass goes layer by layer from the input to the output.

Derivation
\[\begin{align*} s_i &= \sum_j w_{ij}\,a_j + b_i \why{pre-activation} \\ a_i &= f(s_i) \why{activation} \\ \mathbf{a}^{[l]} &= f\bigl(W^{[l]}\mathbf{a}^{[l-1]} + \mathbf{b}^{[l]}\bigr),\quad \mathbf{a}^{[0]} = \mathbf{x} \why{one layer; output $\hat{\mathbf{y}} = \mathbf{a}^{[L]}$} \end{align*}\]

Summary: Weighted sum, plus bias, through a non-linearity.

Source: DLNN script WS24, Eqs. (3.2)–(3.3), (4.59)–(4.62).

B7 A deep network with and without activation functions?ASKED BEFORE

Schlüter

Core. Without non-linear activations the whole network collapses to one linear model.

Explain. A product of linear maps is again linear. So depth adds nothing, and the network cannot even solve XOR. The non-linearity is what makes depth useful.

Derivation
\[\begin{align*} \mathbf{a}^{[2]} &= W^{[2]}\bigl(W^{[1]}\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{b}^{[1]}\bigr) + \mathbf{b}^{[2]} \why{two layers, no activation} \\ &= \bigl(W^{[2]}W^{[1]}\bigr)\mathbf{x} + \bigl(W^{[2]}\mathbf{b}^{[1]} + \mathbf{b}^{[2]}\bigr) \why{multiply out} \\ &= W\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{b} \why{one linear layer} \end{align*}\]

Summary: No activation $\to$ one linear model. No XOR.

Source: DLNN script WS24, Sec. 4.5.

B8 Universal function approximation theorem.ASKED BEFORE

Lehner

Core. One hidden layer with enough units can approximate any continuous function on a compact set.

Explain. The activation must be non-polynomial, for example a sigmoid. The theorem says the network can represent the function. It does not say how many units we need, and it does not say that gradient descent will find the weights.

Summary: One hidden layer, any continuous function. Existence, not learnability.

Backpropagation and the delta error

This is the most asked topic. Practise writing it until it takes two minutes.

B9 Explain backpropagation and define the delta error.ASKED BEFORE

Hochreiter, Klambauer, Kofler (several students)

Core. Backprop gets all gradients with one forward and one backward pass, and the delta is the derivative of the loss with respect to a pre-activation.

Explain. First the forward pass stores all $s$ and $a$. Then the deltas are computed from the output back to the input. The gradient of a weight is the delta of the unit above times the activation of the unit below. The cost is linear in the number of weights.

Derivation
\[\begin{align*} \delta_i &:= \frac{\partial L}{\partial s_i} \why{definition} \\ \frac{\partial L}{\partial w_{ij}} &= \frac{\partial L}{\partial s_i}\,\frac{\partial s_i}{\partial w_{ij}} \why{chain rule: $w_{ij}$ acts only through $s_i$} \\ &= \delta_i\,a_j \why{$s_i = \sum_j w_{ij}a_j + b_i$} \\ w_{ij} &\leftarrow w_{ij} - \eta\,\delta_i\,a_j \why{gradient step} \end{align*}\]

Summary: $\delta$ = dLoss/d(pre-activation). Gradient = $\delta$ above $\times$ activation below.

Source: DLNN script WS24, Sec. 4.6.2, Eqs. (4.63)–(4.68).

B10 Write the recursive formula for the deltas.ASKED BEFORE

Klambauer, Kofler (“delta error of the $l$-th layer”)

Core. The delta of a hidden unit is the weighted sum of the deltas above, times the slope of its own activation.

Explain. A hidden unit $j$ affects the loss only through the units $i$ of the next layer. So we sum over those paths. In matrix form we multiply by the weight matrix and then by the derivatives of the activations.

Derivation
\[\begin{align*} \delta_j &= \frac{\partial L}{\partial s_j} = \sum_i \frac{\partial L}{\partial s_i}\,\frac{\partial s_i}{\partial a_j}\,\frac{\partial a_j}{\partial s_j} \why{chain rule over all units $i$ above} \\ &= \sum_i \delta_i\,w_{ij}\,f'(s_j) \why{$\partial s_i/\partial a_j = w_{ij}$, $\partial a_j/\partial s_j = f'(s_j)$} \\ \bm{\delta}^{[l-1]} &= \mathrm{diag}\bigl(f'(\mathbf{s}^{[l-1]})\bigr)\,W^{[l]\mathsf{T}}\,\bm{\delta}^{[l]} \why{layer form} \\ \frac{\partial L}{\partial W^{[l]}} &= \bm{\delta}^{[l]}\,\mathbf{a}^{[l-1]\mathsf{T}} \why{outer product} \end{align*}\]

Summary: Deltas flow down: multiply by $W^{\mathsf T}$, then by $f'$.

Source: DLNN script WS24, Eqs. (4.71), (4.77)–(4.84).

B11 What is the delta at the output with softmax and cross-entropy?ASKED BEFORE

Hochreiter (softmax + cross-entropy)

Core. It is simply prediction minus label.

Explain. The same clean result holds for a linear output with squared loss and for a sigmoid with binary cross-entropy. That is why these pairs are used together.

Derivation
\[\begin{align*} a_k &= \frac{e^{s_k}}{\sum_m e^{s_m}},\quad L = -\sum_k y_k\log a_k \why{softmax and cross-entropy} \\ \frac{\partial a_k}{\partial s_i} &= a_k\,(\mathbb{1}[k=i] - a_i) \why{derivative of the softmax} \\ \frac{\partial L}{\partial s_i} &= -\sum_k \frac{y_k}{a_k}\,a_k\,(\mathbb{1}[k=i] - a_i) \why{chain rule} \\ &= -y_i + a_i\sum_k y_k = a_i - y_i \why{one-hot label: $\sum_k y_k = 1$} \end{align*}\]

Summary: Matching pair $\to$ $\delta$ = prediction $-$ label.

Source: DLNN script WS24, Eq. (4.70), Table 4.1.

B12 Vanishing and exploding gradients. Who found it?ASKED BEFORE

Schedl committee (“backpropagation, vanishing gradients”)

Core. The delta is multiplied by one Jacobian per layer, so over many layers it shrinks or grows exponentially.

Explain. With the sigmoid the slope is at most 0.25, so the deltas vanish. Sepp Hochreiter analysed this in his diploma thesis in 1991, here in Linz. The fixes are ReLU or SELU, good initialization, normalization, residual connections and, for sequences, the LSTM.

Derivation
\[\begin{align*} \bm{\delta}^{[1]} &= \Bigl(\prod_{l=2}^{L} \mathrm{diag}(f'(\mathbf{s}^{[l-1]}))\,W^{[l]\mathsf{T}}\Bigr)\bm{\delta}^{[L]} \why{apply the recursion $L-1$ times} \\ \lVert\bm{\delta}^{[1]}\rVert &\le \bigl(\max\lvert f'\rvert\cdot\max_l\lVert W^{[l]}\rVert\bigr)^{L-1}\lVert\bm{\delta}^{[L]}\rVert \why{norm of a product} \\ &\le \bigl(0.25\,\lVert W\rVert\bigr)^{L-1}\lVert\bm{\delta}^{[L]}\rVert \why{sigmoid: $f' \le 0.25$} \end{align*}\]

If $0.25\lVert W\rVert < 1$, the delta vanishes exponentially with depth.

Summary: Product of Jacobians. Hochreiter 1991. Fix: ReLU/SELU, init, ResNet, LSTM.

B13 Write gradient descent. Why mini-batches?ASKED BEFORE

Schlüter, Klambauer

Core. We move the weights a small step against the gradient of the empirical risk.

Explain. The full batch gives the exact gradient but is expensive. One sample is cheap but noisy. A mini-batch is the compromise: a good estimate, fast on a GPU, and its noise helps to leave sharp minima.

Derivation
\[\begin{align*} \mathbf{w} &\leftarrow \mathbf{w} - \eta\,\nabla_{\mathbf{w}}\Remp(\mathbf{w}) \why{full-batch gradient descent} \\ \nabla\Remp &\approx \frac{1}{\lvert B\rvert}\sum_{n\in B}\nabla L_n \why{mini-batch $B$: unbiased estimate} \end{align*}\]

Summary: Step against the gradient. Mini-batch = cheap, parallel, useful noise.

Derivation

B18 Why does initialization matter? What is batch normalization?PREDICTED

Core. Random weights break the symmetry, and their scale must keep the variance constant through the layers.

Recurrent networks and LSTM

B21 Explain the forward pass of a recurrent network. How is it trained?ASKED BEFORE

J. Küng committee, Lehner (“main idea of RNNs”)

Core. An RNN reads a sequence step by step and keeps a hidden state that carries the past.

Explain. The same weights are used at every step. It is trained with backpropagation through time: unfold the network over the sequence and backpropagate as in a deep network with shared weights. The number of parameters does not grow with the length of the sequence.

Derivation
\[\begin{align*} \mathbf{s}(t) &= W\mathbf{x}(t) + R\,\mathbf{a}(t-1),\quad \mathbf{a}(t) = f(\mathbf{s}(t)) \why{forward pass} \\ \bm\delta(t) &= \mathrm{diag}\bigl(f'(\mathbf{s}(t))\bigr)\Bigl(V^{\mathsf{T}}\frac{\partial L(t)}{\partial\hat{\mathbf{y}}(t)} + R^{\mathsf{T}}\bm\delta(t+1)\Bigr) \why{delta from the output and from the next step} \\ \frac{\partial L}{\partial R} &= \sum_t \bm\delta(t)\,\mathbf{a}(t-1)^{\mathsf{T}} \why{shared weights: sum over time} \end{align*}\]

Summary: Same weights every step. Hidden state = memory. BPTT = backprop on the unfolded net.

Source: LSTM lecture WS24 (Hochreiter).

B22 Why do plain RNNs fail on long sequences, and how does LSTM fix it?PREDICTED

LSTM lecture (Hochreiter); vanishing gradients were asked

Core. The delta is multiplied by the recurrent Jacobian at every step, so it vanishes; the LSTM cell is updated by addition instead.

Explain. The LSTM (Hochreiter and Schmidhuber 1997) has a memory cell with a constant error carousel. Gates decide what to write, what to keep and what to read. With the forget gate near one, the error flows back through time without shrinking.

Derivation
\[\begin{align*} \mathbf{c}(t) &= \mathbf{f}(t)\odot\mathbf{c}(t-1) + \mathbf{i}(t)\odot\mathbf{z}(t) \why{additive cell update} \\ \mathbf{h}(t) &= \mathbf{o}(t)\odot\tanh\bigl(\mathbf{c}(t)\bigr) \why{output gate reads the cell} \\ \frac{\partial\mathbf{c}(t)}{\partial\mathbf{c}(t-1)} &= \mathrm{diag}\bigl(\mathbf{f}(t)\bigr) \why{direct path through the cell} \\ &\approx I \why{forget gate near one: error carried unchanged} \end{align*}\]

Summary: Additive cell = constant error carousel. Gates: input, forget, output.

Source: LSTM lecture WS24, slides 6–10.

B24 Explain attention and the Transformer.ASKED BEFORE

Rekabsaz, Lehner, Klambauer (self-attention matrices)

Core. Every token takes a weighted mean of all tokens, and the weights come from query times key.

Explain. Each token makes a query, a key and a value by linear maps. The softmax of query times key gives the weights, and the output mixes the values with them. A Transformer block is multi-head self-attention plus an MLP, each with a residual connection and layer normalization.

Derivation
\[\begin{align*} Q &= XW_Q,\ K = XW_K,\ V = XW_V \why{linear maps of the tokens} \\ \mathrm{Att}(Q,K,V) &= \mathrm{softmax}\Bigl(\frac{QK^{\mathsf{T}}}{\sqrt{d_k}}\Bigr)V \why{weights per row sum to one} \\ \mathrm{Var}(\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{k}) &= \sum_{m=1}^{d_k}\mathrm{Var}(q_m k_m) = d_k \why{independent entries of variance 1} \\ \mathrm{Var}\bigl(\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{k}/\sqrt{d_k}\bigr) &= 1 \why{so the softmax does not saturate} \end{align*}\]

Summary: Query meets key $\to$ softmax weights $\to$ mix the values.

B27 Explain the support vector machine.ASKED BEFORE

Kofler, March 2026 committee

Core. The SVM finds the separating hyperplane with the largest margin.

Explain. The margin is $2/\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert$, so maximizing it means minimizing $\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert^2$. Derivation
\[\begin{align*} &\min_{\mathbf{w},b,\bm\xi}\ \tfrac12\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert^2 + C\sum_n\xi_n \why{large margin, few violations} \\ &\text{s.t.}\ \ y_n(\mathbf{w}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{x}_n + b) \ge 1 - \xi_n,\ \ \xi_n \ge 0 \why{every point on the right side, up to slack} \\ &\max_{\bm\alpha}\ \sum_n\alpha_n - \tfrac12\sum_{n,m}\alpha_n\alpha_m y_n y_m\,\mathbf{x}_n^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{x}_m \why{dual, $0 \le \alpha_n \le C$, $\sum_n\alpha_n y_n = 0$} \end{align*}\]

Summary: Max margin. Dual has only dot products.

B31 Derive the REINFORCE loss. (Asked by Prof. Hochreiter.)ASKED BEFORE

Core. The Reynolds number compares inertia with viscous forces.

Explain. Pipe flow is laminar below about 2300 and turbulent above about 4000. Other numbers: the Péclet number compares convection with diffusion, the Prandtl number momentum diffusion with heat diffusion, the Froude number inertia with gravity, and the Richardson number buoyancy with inertia.

Numbers. My case: $Re \approx 99$, laminar. $Ri = 1.84$.

Derivation
\[\begin{align*} Re &= \frac{UL}{\nu},\quad Pe = \frac{UL}{\Gamma},\quad Pr = \frac{\nu}{\alpha},\quad Ri = \frac{g'L}{U^2} \why{ratios of effects} \end{align*}\]

Summary: Re: inertia/viscosity. Pe: convection/diffusion. Ri: buoyancy/inertia.

C25 Governing equations and the material derivative.PREDICTED

Core. Mass, momentum and energy are conserved, and the material derivative links the moving parcel with the fixed control volume.

Explain. The Lagrangian view follows a fluid parcel. The Eulerian view looks at a fixed point. The material derivative is the rate of change seen by the moving parcel.

Derivation
\[\begin{align*} \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\phi\bigl(\mathbf{x}(t),t\bigr) &= \frac{\partial\phi}{\partial t} + \sum_i\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial x_i}\frac{\mathrm{d}x_i}{\mathrm{d}t} \why{chain rule along the path} \\ &= \frac{\partial\phi}{\partial t} + \mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla\phi = \frac{\mathrm{D}\phi}{\mathrm{D}t} \why{the parcel moves with $\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}/\mathrm{d}t = \mathbf{u}$} \\ \frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t} + \nabla\cdot(\rho\mathbf{u}) &= 0 \why{mass} \\ \frac{\partial(\rho\mathbf{u})}{\partial t} + \nabla\cdot(\rho\mathbf{u}\mathbf{u}) &= -\nabla p + \nabla\cdot\bm\tau + \rho\mathbf{g} \why{momentum} \end{align*}\]

Summary: Change in time + convection = pressure + viscous + body force.

Core. First-order upwind is stable but adds false diffusion; central differencing is more accurate but can oscillate.

Explain. Upwind takes the value from upstream. Its truncation error acts like an extra diffusivity that smears sharp gradients. Central differencing oscillates when the cell Péclet number is above 2. Derivation
\[\begin{align*} c_{i-1} &= c_i - \Delta x\,\partial_x c + \tfrac12\Delta x^2\,\partial_{xx}c - \dots \why{Taylor series} \\ \frac{c_i - c_{i-1}}{\Delta x} &= \partial_x c - \tfrac12\Delta x\,\partial_{xx}c + \dots \why{upwind difference, $u > 0$} \\ \partial_t c + u\,\partial_x c &= \tfrac12 u\Delta x\,\partial_{xx}c + \dots \why{what upwind really solves} \\ \Gamma_{\mathrm{num}} &= \tfrac12\,u\,\Delta x \why{numerical diffusivity} \end{align*}\]

Summary: Upwind: stable, diffusive, $\Gamma_{\mathrm{num}} = u\Delta x/2$. Central: accurate, oscillates if $Pe > 2$.

Explain. SIMPLE stands for Semi-Implicit Method for Pressure-Linked Equations (Patankar). The velocity correction depends on the pressure correction.
