01

Title

My thesis simulates a warm jet rising through cold liquid with a flow recorded once, and a recorded flow can carry such a temperature if the correction acts at the thermocline and the database holds the flow regime.

Script

Good morning, and thank you for coming. My thesis is called Data-based Numerical Simulation of Buoyant Jet Flow, which means that I simulate a warm jet rising through cold liquid without solving the flow again, because the flow comes from data recorded in one earlier solver run. The difficulty is that the warm liquid is no passive passenger. The temperature changes the flow, and the flow carries the temperature. What I found fits in one line. A recorded flow can carry a temperature that drives the flow, if the correction acts at the thermocline and the database holds the flow regime. I worked in two stages, first a small closed configuration where every error can be traced to its source, then a laboratory vessel. In the closed configuration the liquid rests at 293 kelvin. A warm jet at 333 kelvin enters for 315 seconds, and I followed the run to 599 seconds. The warm liquid gathers under the lid, and the thin border between warm and cold is the thermocline. I recorded this flow once, and the reason for doing so lies in two very different clocks.

02

Two time scales

The flow settles in seconds and the temperature takes minutes, so the flow is worth storing and the temperature worth following.

Script

In a thermal store or a reactor, the flow finds its pattern within seconds, while the temperature needs minutes to spread. In this case the jet crosses the domain in about 4 seconds, and filling takes 169 seconds. Conventional CFD ignores this difference. It takes five time steps for every second of process, each with thirty inner iterations. For one run that is fine, but a digital twin that follows a live process needs its answer within minutes. So the fast flow is worth storing and the slow temperature worth following, and storing a flow is exactly what recurrence CFD does.

Casual

Here is the reason. Think of a thermal storage system, a buoyant jet or a reactor. In all of them the flow pattern settles within seconds. The temperature, however, needs minutes to spread. Conventional CFD does not care about this difference. It keeps resolving the fast flow at every step of the slow process. For a single run that is acceptable. But an engineer who compares many operating schedules, or a digital twin that follows a live process, needs answers within minutes. So what if we compute the flow only once, and then follow the temperature? How would that work in practice?

03

The database

One solver run is stored and replayed in minutes, but the replay remembers how the flow moved and forgets why.

Script

In recurrence CFD the database keeps the flow as cell-to-cell shifts, one map for every second of the run. The replay chains these maps and adds a face exchange between neighbouring cells, for the mixing the shifts cannot resolve. Classical recurrence CFD jumps between states that repeat. My flow does not repeat clearly, so the replay plays the 600 frames in order, and recorded transport still replaces the flow solution. The reference took 569 minutes of solver time, and a replay takes about 2 minutes. For the laboratory vessel with the lid feed, 7.4 days became 8.5 minutes, about 1250 times faster, all on one laptop. The replay remembers how the flow moved, but it has no idea why, and that missing why is buoyancy.

Casual

This is recurrence CFD. A single solver run is stored as a database of cell-to-cell shifts, which is the memory of the flow. The replay reads these shifts in sequence and adds a face exchange for the mixing it cannot resolve. It transports only the temperature, and it never solves the flow equations again. So what does this save? In the closed configuration, the reference needed 569 minutes of solver time, and one replay takes about two minutes. For the laboratory vessel with the feed at the lid, the reference took 7.4 days, and the replay takes 8.5 minutes, about 1250 times faster. All times were measured on one laptop with four cores and 16 gigabytes of memory. The speed is real. But here is the catch. The replay remembers how the flow moved, and not why it moved. So can we still trust the answer? Let us see what the replay leaves out.

04

The open point

Warm liquid expands and rises, the reference keeps this buoyancy and the replay does not, which leaves three weak points.

Script

Warm liquid expands, so the same mass takes up more room. Between 293 and 333 kelvin the density drops from 998.2 to 983.2 kilograms per cubic metre. A warm parcel is therefore lighter than the cold liquid it pushes aside, and gravity leaves it a net upward force. For the full 40 kelvin this buoyancy is about 0.15 metres per second squared, only 1.5 per cent of gravity. A change this small allows the Boussinesq form, where the density stays constant except in the buoyancy term of the momentum equation. The replay keeps only the energy equation, driven by the recorded velocity, so the temperature can never push back on the flow. That left me with three weak points. The coupling runs one way, the face-exchange strength f s d is a free constant that I set by hand, and a cooled lid acts locally while the database stays fixed. To find out which of them matters, I set up a simple test design.

Measures

Five measures give one common ruler, because one number can hide a large error somewhere else.

Script

I used all five, because a single number can hide a large error somewhere else. The first question was about the mixing.

Standard replay

No setting of the mixing strength satisfies both errors, because the outlet sits just below the thermocline.

Script

The standard replay smeared the thermocline, so my first suspect was the mixing. I ran seven values of the face-exchange strength. The error of the mean temperature was smallest at low f s d, and the error of the height of the heat was smallest near 0.5. On the right, no run reaches the corner where both errors are small. The reason sits at the outlet, just below the thermocline. When numerical diffusion thickens the thermocline, warm liquid reaches the outlet and leaves. Stronger mixing therefore makes the replay too warm during the inflow and too cold after it. A dial that blurs the whole field could not fix this, so I needed something that acts at the thermocline itself.

08

Extended replay

A bounded step moves temperature up the gradient but never past what the neighbours allow.

Script

That something is a bounded anti-diffusive correction, applied inside every replay step, right after the face exchange. Heat moves up the local gradient, but never past the values of the neighbouring cells. It works like sharpening a blurred photograph without inventing a colour that no neighbour has. The limit comes from a limiter of the Zalesak type. The correction conserves heat and stays bounded for a correction strength s up to one half, and a direction weight keeps the exchange mostly across the thermocline. On the model front, plain negative diffusion soon leaves the data range, while the limited correction stays inside it. The real test was the replayed field.

A database recorded under an adiabatic lid gives 63 percent of the heat loss, and one recorded under cooling gives 98 percent.

Script

Casual

Some leads remain open. I chose the correction strength by parameter studies for each configuration. A small network could predict the local strength from the replayed field. It would act inside the limiter, so conservation and the local bounds are kept. A loss based on the residual of the energy equation could use sparse sensor data, such as the vessel thermocouples.
