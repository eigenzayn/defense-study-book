01

Title

A buoyant jet can be replayed from a recording if the temperature is still computed, and this talk tests that claim in two stages.

Script

Good morning. My thesis is called Data-based Numerical Simulation of Buoyant Jet Flow. A buoyant jet is driven by its own momentum and by the lift of warm liquid. Numerical simulation means the temperature is still computed on a mesh, cell by cell, with its energy balance. Data-based means the flow comes from a database recorded in one solver run, and we replay it instead of solving it again. The thesis claims that a recorded flow can carry a temperature that drives the flow, if the correction acts at the thermocline and the database holds the flow regime. I tested this in two stages, first a small closed configuration, then the laboratory vessel. In the closed configuration the liquid rests at 293 kelvin. A warm jet at 333 kelvin enters for 315 seconds, and I followed the run to 599 seconds. A warm layer settles on top, and its border with the cold liquid is the thermocline. I recorded this flow once, and the next slide shows why.

Casual

The temperature changes the flow, and the flow carries the temperature. So can a recording of the flow really transport the temperature for us? To find out, I took two steps. First, a small closed configuration with a laminar flow, where I can follow every error back to its source. Then a laboratory vessel, to see whether the answer still holds in a real rig. Two time scales

The flow settles in seconds and the temperature takes minutes, so the flow is worth storing and the temperature worth following.

Script

In thermal storage, in a buoyant jet, in a reactor, the flow pattern settles within seconds, but the temperature needs minutes to spread. Here the jet crosses the domain in about 4 seconds, while filling takes 169. Conventional CFD takes five time steps per second of process, with thirty inner iterations each, so it solves the fast flow again and again. That is fine for one run. A digital twin needs answers within minutes. So the flow is worth storing and the temperature worth following. The next slide shows how I stored it.

Casual

Here is the reason. Think of a thermal storage system, a buoyant jet or a reactor. In all of them the flow pattern settles within seconds. The temperature, however, needs minutes to spread. Conventional CFD does not care about this difference. It keeps resolving the fast flow at every step of the slow process. For a single run that is acceptable. But an engineer who compares many operating schedules, or a digital twin that follows a live process, needs answers within minutes. So what if we compute the flow only once, and then follow the temperature? How would that work in practice?

03

The database

One solver run is stored and replayed in minutes, but the replay remembers how the flow moved and forgets why.

Script

The method is recurrence CFD. The database holds the flow as cell-to-cell shifts, one map for every second. The replay chains these shifts and adds a face exchange for the mixing they cannot resolve. This flow does not repeat clearly, so the replay plays the frames in order. It is still recurrence CFD, because recorded transport replaces the flow solution. The reference needed 569 minutes of solver time, and a replay takes about 2 minutes. For the laboratory vessel with the lid feed, 7.4 days became 8.5 minutes, about 1250 times faster, on one laptop. The replay remembers how the flow moved and forgets why. That turned my attention to the physics the recording leaves out.

Casual

This is recurrence CFD. A single solver run is stored as a database of cell-to-cell shifts, which is the memory of the flow. The replay reads these shifts in sequence and adds a face exchange for the mixing it cannot resolve. It transports only the temperature, and it never solves the flow equations again. So what does this save? In the closed configuration, the reference needed 569 minutes of solver time, and one replay takes about two minutes. For the laboratory vessel with the feed at the lid, the reference took 7.4 days, and the replay takes 8.5 minutes, about 1250 times faster. All times were measured on one laptop with four cores and 16 gigabytes of memory. The speed is real. But here is the catch. The replay remembers how the flow moved, and not why it moved. So can we still trust the answer? Let us see what the replay leaves out.

04

The open point

Warm liquid expands and rises, the reference keeps this buoyancy and the replay does not, which leaves three weak points.

Script

The physics left out is buoyancy. Warm liquid expands, so the same mass fills more volume. Between 293 and 333 kelvin the density falls from 998.2 to 983.2 kilograms per cubic metre. A warm parcel weighs less than the cold liquid it pushes aside, so gravity leaves a net upward force. For the full 40 kelvin it is about 0.15 metres per second squared, 1.5 per cent of gravity. That is small enough for the Boussinesq form, which keeps density constant except in the buoyancy term. The reference solves all three equations together. The replay keeps only the energy equation, with the recorded velocity, so the temperature never pushes back on the flow. This left three weak points. The coupling runs in one direction, the face-exchange strength f s d is a free constant that I set by hand, and a cooled lid acts locally while the database stays fixed.

Let us start with the physics of the reference. Over this temperature range the density changes by only 1.5 per cent, so the Boussinesq form applies. The reference solves all of them together. The replay uses only the last one, with the recorded motion. So it carries the temperature, but the temperature can never push back on the velocity. That gap leaves three places where the replay may go wrong. The coupling runs in one direction only. The strength of the face exchange, f s d, is a free constant that I set by hand. And a cooled lid acts locally, while the database stays fixed. Which of the three matters most? We need a plan to find out.

Standard replay

No setting of the mixing strength satisfies both errors, because the outlet sits just below the thermocline.

Script

That result sent me looking for something that acts at the thermocline itself.

Extended replay

A bounded step moves temperature up the gradient but never past what the neighbours allow.

Script

The remedy is a correction inside each replay step. After the face exchange, heat moves up the local gradient, but only within the bounds set by the neighbouring cells. It works like sharpening a blurred photograph without inventing a temperature that no neighbour has. This is a bounded anti-diffusive correction with a limiter of the Zalesak type. It conserves heat and stays bounded for a correction strength s up to one half. A direction weight keeps the step mostly across the thermocline. In the closed configuration the recorded diffusivity is so large that it saturates the cap. On the slide, a model front with negative diffusion leaves the data range, while the limited correction stays inside. I then looked at three fields side by side.

A database recorded under an adiabatic lid gives 63 percent of the heat loss, and one recorded under cooling gives 98 percent.

Script

A non-passive scalar can be replayed if the correction acts at the thermocline and the database contains the flow regime, within stated limits.

Script

A recorded flow can carry a temperature that drives the flow, if the correction acts at the thermocline and the database holds the flow regime. There are five findings. Future work

The correction strength is the first lead for learning, and none of these leads was tested in the thesis.

Script

I chose the correction strength by parameter studies, so it is the first candidate for learning. A small network could predict it locally inside the limiter, which keeps conservation and the local bounds. A loss built on the residual of the energy equation could use sparse sensor data, such as the vessel thermocouples. Separate databases could cover flow regimes. Two steps need no learning. A shorter step or an implicit exchange would let the recorded diffusivity act, and a second mesh with measurements would test the references. None of these leads was tested in this thesis.

Casual

Some leads remain open. I chose the correction strength by parameter studies for each configuration. A small network could predict the local strength from the replayed field. It would act inside the limiter, so conservation and the local bounds are kept. A loss based on the residual of the energy equation could use sparse sensor data, such as the vessel thermocouples. And separate databases for different flow regimes could be selected from the replayed temperature. Two further steps need no learning. We could let the recorded diffusivity act through a shorter step or an implicit exchange. And we should validate against measurements in a closed configuration and on a second mesh. None of this was tested in the thesis. Larger field-to-field models would need many paired states, and they give no bounds. So this is a map for future work, and not a result.
