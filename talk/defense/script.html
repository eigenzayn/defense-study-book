01

Title

My thesis asks whether a recorded flow can carry a temperature that drives the flow, and the answer depends on the correction and on the database.

Script

Good morning, and thank you for being here. My thesis is called Data-based Numerical Simulation of Buoyant Jet Flow. A buoyant jet is driven by its own momentum and by the lift of warm liquid. Numerical simulation means that the temperature is still computed on a mesh, cell by cell. Data-based means that the flow is not computed again. It is replayed from a database recorded in one solver run. The thesis shows that a recorded flow can carry a temperature that drives the flow, if the correction acts at the thermocline and the database holds the flow regime. I tested this in two steps. First, a small closed configuration where I can follow every error to its source. Then a laboratory vessel. Here is the closed configuration. The liquid is at rest at 293 kelvin. A warm jet at 333 kelvin enters for 315 seconds, and we follow the run until 599 seconds. A warm layer settles on the cold one, and we call their border the thermocline. This is the flow we record, just once. But why record a flow at all?

Two time scales

The flow settles in seconds and the temperature needs minutes, so the flow is worth storing and the temperature worth following.

Script

03

The database

One solver run is stored and replayed in minutes, but the replay remembers how the flow moved and not why.

Script

The method is recurrence CFD. The database stores the flow as cell-to-cell shifts, one map for every second. The replay chains these shifts and adds a face exchange for the mixing they cannot resolve. Classical recurrence CFD jumps between states that repeat. This flow does not repeat clearly, so the replay plays the 600 frames in order. Recorded transport still replaces the flow solution. In the closed configuration, the reference needed 569 minutes, and a replay takes about two minutes. For the laboratory vessel with the lid feed, 7.4 days become 8.5 minutes, about 1250 times faster. All times come from one laptop. The replay remembers how the flow moved. It does not know why the flow moved. So what does it leave out?

The open point

Warm liquid expands and becomes lighter, so it rises. The reference keeps this buoyancy and the replay does not.

Script

Warm liquid expands, so the same mass fills a larger volume. The density falls from 998.2 kilograms per cubic metre at 293 kelvin to 983.2 at 333 kelvin. A warm parcel weighs less than the cold liquid it pushes aside, so gravity leaves a net upward force. For 40 kelvin, this buoyancy is about 0.15 metres per second squared, 1.5 per cent of gravity. The density changes by only 1.5 per cent, so the Boussinesq form applies. The density is constant, except in one buoyancy term of the momentum equation. The reference solves all its equations together. The replay keeps only the energy equation, driven by the recorded velocity. The temperature is carried, and it never pushes back on the velocity. This leaves three weak points. The coupling runs in one direction only. The face-exchange strength f s d is a free constant that I set by hand. A cooled lid acts locally, while the database stays fixed. Which of the three matters most?

Standard replay

No setting of the mixing strength satisfies both errors, because the outlet sits just below the thermocline.

Script

The first puzzle is a smeared thermocline in the standard replay. My first guess was the mixing. So I tested seven values of the face-exchange strength, f s d, and watched two errors. The error of the mean temperature is smallest at low f s d. The error of the height of the heat is smallest near 0.5. The reason is the outlet, which lies just below the thermocline. Numerical diffusion thickens the thermocline, and more heat leaves through the outlet. So stronger mixing makes the replay too warm during the inflow and too cold after it. This one dial blurs one picture while it sharpens another. What could act at the thermocline itself?

Extended replay

A bounded step moves temperature up the gradient but never past what the neighbours allow.

Script

The remedy is a bounded anti-diffusive correction. Inside each replay step, after the face exchange, heat moves up the local gradient. In short, heat moves up the gradient, but never past what the neighbouring cells allow. It is like sharpening a blurred photograph without inventing a temperature that no neighbour has. This is a limiter of the Zalesak type. It conserves heat, and it stays bounded for a strength s up to one half. A vertical direction weight keeps the step mostly across the thermocline. In the closed configuration, the recorded diffusivity is so large that it saturates the cap. Does it really sharpen the layer? Three fields will show it.

Cooled lid

A database recorded under an adiabatic lid gives 63 percent of the heat loss, and one recorded under cooling gives 98 percent.

Script

Now we cool the lid. A wall term on the top row of cells models it, q equals h A times T minus T ambient. Here h is 100 watts per square metre per kelvin, and T ambient is 293 kelvin. The density rule now works in reverse. Cooled liquid becomes denser and sinks, so the cooling changes the flow itself. I first replayed this on the database recorded under an adiabatic lid. It removed only 63 per cent of the reference heat loss. Heat leaves through the lid only as fast as the flow brings warm liquid to it, and this recording holds no sinking motion. So the cause sits in the recording. I recorded the database again, this time under cooling. Now the standard replay removes 98 per cent of the reference heat loss. A database must hold the flow regime that we ask it to replay.

Some leads remain open. I chose the correction strength by parameter studies for each configuration. A small network could predict the local strength from the replayed field.
