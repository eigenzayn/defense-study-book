01

Title

The title in three parts: a buoyant jet, still a numerical simulation, but driven by a flow recorded once. Can such a recording carry a temperature that changes the flow?

Script

Good morning, and thank you for being here. My thesis is called Data-based Numerical Simulation of Buoyant Jet Flow. Let me read that title from the end. A buoyant jet is a jet driven by two things, its own momentum and the lift of warm liquid. Numerical simulation means that the temperature is still computed on a mesh, cell by cell, with its energy balance. And data-based means that the flow itself is not computed again. It comes from data, a database recorded from one solver run, and we replay it whenever we need it. There is one difficulty. The temperature changes the flow, and the flow carries the temperature. So can a recording of the flow really transport such a temperature? I answered this in two steps. First, a small closed configuration with a laminar flow, where I can follow every error back to its source. Then a laboratory vessel, to see whether the answer still holds in a real rig. Here is the closed configuration. The liquid inside is at rest at 293 kelvin. A warm jet at 333 kelvin enters for 315 seconds, and we follow the run until 599 seconds. The jet bends upwards and spreads under the lid. A warm layer settles on top of the cold one, and we call the border between them the thermocline. This is the flow we will record, just once. But why would anyone want to record a flow?

02

Two time scales

The flow settles in seconds and the temperature takes minutes, so the flow is worth storing and the temperature worth following.

Script

Here is the reason. Think of a thermal storage system, a buoyant jet or a reactor. In all of them the flow pattern settles within seconds. The temperature, however, needs minutes to spread. Conventional CFD does not care about this difference. It keeps resolving the fast flow at every step of the slow process. For a single run that is acceptable. But an engineer who compares many operating schedules, or a digital twin that follows a live process, needs answers within minutes. So the slow part, the temperature, is worth following, and the fast part, the flow, is worth storing. But how can a stored flow move the temperature?

03

The database

One solver run is stored and replayed in minutes, but the replay remembers how the flow moved and not why.

Script

This is recurrence CFD. The database stores the flow as cell-to-cell shifts, one map for every second. The replay chains these shifts, and adds a face exchange for the mixing that the shifts cannot resolve. Classical recurrence CFD jumps between states that repeat. This flow does not repeat clearly, so the replay plays the 600 frames in order. It is still recurrence CFD, because recorded transport replaces the flow solution. So what does this save? In the closed configuration, the reference needed 569 minutes of solver time, and one replay takes about two minutes. For the laboratory vessel with the feed at the lid, the reference took 7.4 days, and the replay takes 8.5 minutes, about 1250 times faster. All times were measured on one laptop with four cores and 16 gigabytes of memory. The speed is real. But the replay remembers how the flow moved, and not why it moved. So what exactly does it leave out?

04

The open point

Warm liquid expands and becomes lighter, so it rises. The reference keeps this buoyancy, the replay does not, and that leaves three weak points.

Script

Why does the warm jet rise? When the liquid warms up, it expands, so the same mass fills a larger volume. Its density falls from 998.2 kilograms per cubic metre at 293 kelvin to 983.2 at 333 kelvin. A warm parcel therefore weighs less than the cold liquid it pushes aside, and gravity leaves a net upward force. For the full difference of 40 kelvin, this buoyancy is about 0.15 metres per second squared, only one and a half per cent of gravity. Because the density changes by only 1.5 per cent, the Boussinesq form applies. The density is constant everywhere, except in one term of the momentum equation, minus g beta times T minus T zero. The reference solves all three equations together. The replay keeps only the energy equation, driven by the recorded velocity. So the temperature is carried, but it never pushes back on the velocity. That gap leaves three places where the replay may go wrong. The coupling runs in one direction only. The strength of the face exchange, f s d, is a free constant that I set by hand. And a cooled lid acts locally, while the database stays fixed. Which of the three matters most? We need a plan to find out.

Script

06

Measures

Five measures give one common ruler, because one number can hide a large error somewhere else.

Script

To judge the answers we need one common ruler, made of five measures. The mean temperature tells us how much heat the domain holds. The height of the heat, z c o g, tells us where that heat sits. And the heterogeneity, delta T mean, tells us how strongly the liquid is layered. The end-profile departure compares the final temperature against height. The thermocline thickness, from 10 to 90 per cent, tells us how sharp the layer is. Why five? Because one number can hide a large error somewhere else. On a time trace, the squared error splits into squared bias plus variance. With this ruler in hand, what does the first question show?

07

Standard replay

No setting of the mixing strength satisfies both errors, because the outlet sits just below the thermocline.

Script

The first question begins with a puzzle. In the standard replay, the thermocline smears. My first guess was the mixing. So I tested seven values of the face-exchange strength, f s d, and watched two errors. The error of the mean temperature is smallest at low f s d. But the error of the height of the heat is smallest near 0.5. The two measures disagree. Why? The outlet lies just below the thermocline. Numerical diffusion thickens the thermocline, and more heat then leaves through the outlet. That is why stronger mixing makes the replay too warm during the inflow and too cold after it. So turning this dial blurs one picture while it sharpens another. What could act at the thermocline itself?

08

Extended replay

A bounded step moves temperature up the gradient but never past what the neighbours allow.

Script

Here is the remedy. Inside each replay step, after the face exchange, heat moves up the local gradient. But only within the bounds set by the neighbouring cells. The formula looks heavy, yet the idea is short. Move heat up the gradient, but never past what the neighbours allow. It is like sharpening a blurred photograph, without inventing a temperature that no neighbour has. This is a limiter of the Zalesak type. It conserves heat, and it stays bounded for a strength s up to one half. A vertical weight keeps the step mostly across the thermocline. In the closed configuration, the recorded diffusivity is so large that it saturates the cap. Does it really sharpen the layer? Let us look at three fields side by side.

11

Cooled lid

A database recorded under an adiabatic lid gives 63 percent of the heat loss, and one recorded under cooling gives 98 percent.

Script

Now we cool the lid. We model the cooling with a wall term on the top row of cells, q equals h A times T minus T ambient. Here h is 100 watts per square metre per kelvin, and T ambient is 293 kelvin. Now the density rule works in reverse. The cooled liquid becomes denser and sinks, so the cooling changes the flow itself. I first replayed this on the database we already had, recorded under an adiabatic lid. The replay removed only 63 per cent of the reference heat loss. But here is the catch. Heat leaves through the lid only as fast as the flow brings warm liquid to it, and this recording holds no sinking motion. So the reason sits in the recording, and not in the replay. I recorded the database again, this time under cooling. Now the standard replay removes 98 per cent of the reference heat loss. A database must contain the flow regime that we ask it to replay.

Answers

A non-passive scalar can be replayed if the correction acts at the thermocline and the database contains the flow regime, within stated limits.

Script

So, can a recorded flow carry a temperature that changes the flow? Yes, if the correction acts at the thermocline and the database contains the flow regime. Let me take the five questions in order. No single face-exchange strength is best. The bounded correction restores the thermocline, and no end field leaves the range of the data. Under a cooled lid, this works only with a database recorded under cooling. The filling error is missing transport, and not a local error. And in the vessel, the operators transfer, but motion that is absent from the database cannot be recovered. These answers have limits. I used one closed configuration, one mesh and one vessel, and the references are simulations. Within these limits, the answer is yes, with care. So where could this go next?

Future work

The correction strength is the first lead for learning, and none of these leads was tested in the thesis.

Script

Some leads remain open. I chose the correction strength by parameter studies for each configuration. A small network could predict the local strength from the replayed field. It would act inside the limiter, so conservation and the local bounds are kept. A loss based on the residual of the energy equation could use sparse sensor data, such as the vessel thermocouples. And separate databases for different flow regimes could be selected from the replayed temperature. Two steps need no learning at all. A shorter step or an implicit exchange would let the recorded diffusivity act, and a second mesh with measurements would test the references. None of this was tested in the thesis. Larger field-to-field models would need many paired states, and they give no bounds. So this is a map for future work, and not a result.
